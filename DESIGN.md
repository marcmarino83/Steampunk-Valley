# Steampunk Valley: Game Design Document

Nov 2, 2026 · @Marc

## Overview

Steampunk Valley is a first-person 3D survival and settlement-building game: a refugee of the fallen Gilded Empire builds a new home in a hidden valley, and every bit of steam power they harness draws the Hollows closer.

- **Genre:** survival, crafting, settlement building, base defense
- **Inspiration:** Medieval Dynasty, without the dynasty system, in a steampunk setting
- **Engine:** Godot 4.7, GDScript, built with help from Claude in VS Code

**Pillars**

1. **Build from nothing.** Start with scrap and sticks; end with a steam-powered settlement.
2. **Progress is risk.** Heat, smoke, and running machinery attract the Hollows, so every upgrade is a trade-off.
3. **A valley worth exploring.** Real-world terrain and ruins that slowly reveal what happened to the Empire.

## Story and setting

The Gilded Empire fell when its constructs stopped obeying; the player is an ordinary refugee who flees to a forgotten valley to start over.

**The Gilded Empire.** An empire of steam and clockwork. Its greatest achievement was the constructs, mechanical soldiers and laborers controlled from a great engine in the capital. Then something went wrong. The constructs turned, the capital fell, and the Empire collapsed in a single season.

**The Hollows.** The rogue constructs that now roam the wilds. They are drawn to heat, smoke, and running machinery.

**The player.** Just a refugee: no special past, no hidden connection to the constructs. Someone who survived the fall and needs a place to live. They reach the valley when the refugee airship carrying them crashes there. The wreck is their first shelter, their first source of scrap, and their first clue, since its log may say what brought it down. The game begins with a falling airship and ends with one the player builds.

**The valley.** A forgotten outpost on the edge of the old Empire, with a ruined airship dock, an abandoned steam town, and rusting machinery in the hills. Quiet, hidden, and a place to begin again.

**How the story unfolds**

- Journals and logs found in the outpost ruins
- Schematics that reveal what the constructs were built for
- Messages from passing airships about the wider world

**Endgame.** Build an airship, fly to the fallen capital, learn what really happened, and perhaps shut down whatever still drives the Hollows.

## Core gameplay loop

Each pass around the loop opens something the player couldn't do before, and each one makes the settlement louder and warmer for the Hollows to find.

Gathering feeds crafting and building; a growing settlement unlocks new tech, which sends the player further out to find tougher resources.

## Opening quests

The first quests teach survival, then lead straight into the settlement game.

1. **Survive:** craft the first tools, build a lean-to, and build a chest.
2. **The ruined castle:** the quest marker moves to a ruined castle, where the first two refugee NPCs are sheltering. They need a home and point the player to a valley down the road, a good place to build.
3. **A house:** build a House there for them.
4. **Food:** once housed, they need food, which leads to the Forager's Hut and learning to assign jobs.

**Settlement progression.** The village grows in this order:

1. **Castle quest:** the first two villagers come from the ruined castle. They're quest villagers: permanent, they never leave.
2. **A house:** they need a House (villager housing) before they move in.
3. **Food:** feeding them leads to the Forager's Hut.
4. **Storage:** the Storehouse (town storage); the Granary later.
5. **Village Center:** a working Storehouse unlocks the Village Center (its Bell Mast), which draws more villagers.
6. **Visitors:** visitors come to the Village Center, and the player recruits them into free villager housing.

Random campsites stay planned (see World and look); **their role is still to decide**.

## Progression

Four eras take the player from scrap to the sky, and each era's technology also raises the Hollows' attention.

| Era | What the player builds | Key resources | Main threat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Scrap and Survival | Stone tools, campfire, rough shelter | Stone, plant fiber, sticks, wood, scrap, berries | Cold, hunger, wolves |
| 2. The Forge | Smelter, forge, workshop, bronze tools | Copper and tin, smelted into bronze; zinc for brass (proposed) | Lone Hollow scouts drawn by forge smoke |
| 3. Steam Power | Sawmill, water wheel, steam pump, steam shovel, walls and steam defenses, clockwork helpers | Iron and coal from underground, steel (proposed), gears, springs, copper plating from Hollows | Hollow raiding parties |
| 4. The Sky | Restored airship dock, trade with passing airships, own airship, clockwork guardians | Aether from capped vents, aluminum for airship frames, construct cores, trade goods | A major Hollow assault drawn by the dock's engines |

**How technology unlocks.** Two routes, mixed:

- **By doing:** chopping, mining, and crafting teach the basics (Medieval Dynasty style). Basic T1 items keep unlocking this way.
- **By discovering:** after T1, the tech tree takes over (see below). Hollow parts studied at the workshop feed it too.

**Tech tree and exploration (after T1)**

- **Schematic fragments** are the research currency, found while exploring.
- **Big points of interest** (the ruined castle, the airship dock, the abandoned workshop) hold whole schematics.
- **The tree:** it shows every era's buildings and tools from the start, each locked until bought with fragments. Some nodes also need an earlier node, or a working building of some kind.
- **Researching** happens at a research table in the Workshop.

**Underground resources.** Iron and coal don't sit on the surface. The player has to craft a prospecting tool to find deposits (ideas: a dowsing rod early, then a brass prospector's scope or seismic thumper), then dig or drill to reach them.

**A building for each tier.** Every tier adds its own production buildings, for example a smelter and forge for bronze, a blast furnace for iron and steel, an aether extractor, and an airship yard.

**Aluminum (proposed lore).** As in the real 1800s, aluminum is rarer than gold because refining it takes enormous power. Aether-powered refining is what finally makes it possible, which ties the last tier together. It is the lightweight metal for airship building.

## Threats

Early danger comes from the land; later danger comes from the Hollows, and the player's own progress is what calls them.

- **Survival pressure:** hunger and stamina meters. Planned: seasons with a harsh winter that needs steam heat, and tools that wear out.
- **Stamina (0–100):** sprinting costs 12 a second, a jump 8, and a tool swing (chopping with the axe, mining with the pickaxe) 6. It comes back at 20 a second once none has been spent for 1 second. At 0 you're winded: no sprinting or swinging until it's back above 15 (walking and jumping still work). Hunger lowers it: well fed you have the full 100 and full regen; starving, at most 50 and half regen, easing in between. A night's sleep refills it. Saved with the game.
- **Warmth (switched off for now):** warmth and the cold are kept in the code but switched off with one project setting, `game/survival/warmth_enabled` (false). While off there's no warmth meter, no cold damage or slowdown, and no freezing, sheltered or indoors messages; fires still give light, and the shelter and closed-room checks still run (beds and settlers will need them). Older saves load without their warmth. **Warmth returns for cold regions (such as the Frost Highlands) and winter**: then it drains at night and in the open, more slowly under a roof, not at all in a closed room, and fires, the fireplace and a held torch warm you; it will need its own meter on the HUD.
- **Wolves:** the first hostile wildlife, in era 1.
- **The Hollows:** drawn to heat, smoke, and running machinery. A campfire barely registers; a forge brings scouts; boilers bring raiding parties; the airship dock brings a major assault.
- **Loot:** defeated Hollows drop gears, copper plating, springs, and eventually construct cores, which feed the tech tree.

**Design rule:** every upgrade is a choice between more power and staying under the radar.

## Combat and magitek

The player fights with a mix of melee, ranged weapons, firearms, and magitek, and the louder, hotter options draw more Hollows.

| Style | Examples | Strength | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Melee | Wrench, cleaver, steam hammer | Needs no ammo, quiet | Close range is risky against big Hollows |
| Ranged | Bow, crossbow, bolt thrower | Silent | Slower, and ammo must be crafted |
| Firearms | Steam pistol, rifle, scattergun | High damage | Loud and hot, so it attracts Hollows; scarce ammo |
| Magitek | Aether gauntlet (lightning), ward lantern, charged blades | Powerful; can stun or disable Hollows | Runs on aether; its glow draws Hollows most of all |

**Aether.** The golden energy that gave the Gilded Empire its name. It powered the great control engine and every construct core, and whatever went wrong with it is what turned the constructs into Hollows. Aether rises from vents in the ground. To collect it, the player caps a vent by placing an extractor building on top of it, so vents are fixed points on the map worth finding, claiming, and defending.

## Settlement and defense

The player starts out doing everything alone and gradually becomes the leader of a working settlement that has to defend itself.

- **Settlers:** the first two come from the castle quest; every one after that is a visitor to the Village Center, recruited into free villager housing (see Settlement progression, under Opening quests).
- **Settler and job plan:** the order is settlers first, then the forager and food, then crafting buildings.
  - **Forager:** a fixed daily amount, split by percentages the player sets (no foraging area).
  - **Food:** each settler eats about 2 food a day.
  - **Crafting buildings (later):** they get NPC workers, and the player can still use their stations. NPCs keep stock targets (e.g. "keep 40 planks") using ingredients from the warehouse.
  - **Still open:** where the player's own crafting ingredients come from, how many workers each building takes, and whether NPCs can craft recipes the player hasn't unlocked.
- **Jobs:** settlers work at a workplace plot (woodcutter, miner, stoker, farmer). At the end of each day their output is calculated and added to the warehouse, so later in the game the player no longer has to grind for materials.
- **Built so far (settlers, first version):** settlers are simple low-poly placeholder figures in refugee clothing (tunic, trousers, scarf, hair or hood or cap, some with a bedroll on their back; colours, height and build vary per settler), each with a random name (`settlers/`).
  - **Quest villagers (stand-in until the castle quest exists):** the first two settlers of a new game. Each morning, while fewer than two have come, if a working villager House has a free bed, one refugee walks in from about 56 m away toward the nearest valley edge and moves into that House: "A refugee named Name has arrived." They carry a permanent quest flag, kept internally and not shown anywhere. They never leave: when their needs aren't met (hungry, or no home) they stop working, make nothing, and say why ("We can't work hungry") until it's fixed. A save from before this marks its two earliest settlers as the quest villagers. F8 (debug builds only; the console's `spawn_settler`) brings an ordinary settler at once, a few steps in front of you, with no home or job.
  - **Visitors (the Village Center):** with a working Village Center, a visitor arrives every 2–4 in-game days (by day, 0.27–0.70 on the sun's clock), at most 3 at a time, walking in from the valley's edge. The Bell Mast's bell rings as they come within 10 m of it (or after 75 s on the way), with an event banner: "A visitor has arrived · Name is waiting at the Village Center".
    - They linger around the mast by day (strolling 2.5–6 m from it, facing it) and rest on the ground by it at night; they need no bed or food. After 1–2 days, if not recruited, they walk away by day and vanish: "Name didn't find a home here and has moved on." If the Village Center stops working, they move on at once.
    - **Talking to one** shows their name and a line of background, with Recruit and Not now. Recruit needs a working villager House with a free bed; otherwise the button reads "Needs a free home". With more than one free House it asks which. A recruited visitor moves into that House as an unemployed settler, under the usual rules (hungry 3 days in a row, they leave).
    - The bell is a placeholder synthesised ship's-bell "ding-ding" (`assets/village_center/bell_mast/bell_ring.wav`), played at the bell and heard across the valley; the game has no other audio yet.
    - **Debug:** the console's `spawn_visitor [count]` brings visitors in front of you (needs a working Village Center), and `skip_days <n>` jumps ahead n mornings, handling each night's meal, each dawn and the visitors' comings and goings.
    - **Where they appear** (quest arrivals, visitors and the console's `spawn_settler` and `spawn_visitor` alike, `SettlerManager.find_safe_spot`): on the visible ground. It looks straight down from 80 m above the terrain, and the first thing hit must be open ground, not a building piece, tree, rock or landmark. The spot must also be dry, no steeper than 32°, clear of the wreck, the castle and rock outcrops, outside building footprints, and at least 1.5 m from any piece. If the first spot fails, it tries rings out to 75 m (8 spots each, each also moved onto the settlers' navigation where it's ready); an arrival then tries beside the House or Village Center. If nothing works, nobody comes then.
    - **Logging:** every attempt goes to the console output, prefixed `[Settlers]`. That includes why no quest villager arrived at dawn (no House, a Player home, a House not working, no free bed with residents/beds), each spot tried and why it was turned down, and where the settler or visitor was placed.
  - **Walking:** Godot navigation, baked at runtime in 32 m tiles around every plot (64 m to spare) and every settler, from the valley's heights (water left out) and the solid shapes on them (pieces, furniture, the workbench, chests, campfires, trees, rocks). Doors are left out, so settlers path through doorways. Once a second each tile's pieces are compared with when it was baked; a tile where something was placed, demolished or moved is baked again, one tile per frame. Settlers step up low ledges (floors on the ground, thresholds up to 0.5 m), open closed doors as they reach them (doors never block them), and close the ones they opened behind them.
  - **Talking:** E aimed at a settler opens their panel (brass plaque): name, home, job, status ("Working at Forager's Hut", "Walking to…", "Heading home", "Sleeping", "Idle: no job", "Can't work: missing roof"), with Assign home and Assign job, each listing the working plots with a free place (plus No home or No job).
  - **Housing rules:** each House plot is either Villager housing (the default) or Player home, set with the "Use as" button in its plot panel. A Player home is never assigned (switching a House to Player home moves its residents out, with a message). Settlers get a home only by being given one (recruited into it, or Assign home in their panel, which lists only villager housing with a free bed), and sleep only in a bed of the House they're assigned to, never any other open bed; nothing grabs a bed or house automatically. Saved with the plot.
  - **Homes and jobs:** a House holds one settler per bed, each sleeping in a bed of their own (beds in the order they were built, residents in the order they arrived). The Forager's and Woodcutter's Huts take 1 worker each (`PlotType.workers`, standing at `PlotType.work_at`: the foraging basket or chopping block); the Workshop and Storehouse come later. The plot panel lists a House's residents and a workplace's worker (with Unassign); the plot tracker has a line for them too.
  - **Daily routine (the sun's clock):** from 0.28 to 0.70 they work, bent over their workplace's furniture, now and then wandering a little inside the plot; in the evening they go home and idle there; from 0.78 to dawn they sleep in their bed. With no job they idle around their home; with no home they sleep by the nearest campfire or fireplace (or where they stand). A workplace that stops working leaves its worker idling there, showing "Can't work: reason".
  - **Daily output (at dawn, 0.27 on the sun's clock):** each working workplace with a worker makes its day's amount into town storage (the working Storehouses; see Storage and builders) while it has room, otherwise into its own storage chest.
    - Forager's Hut (the gatherer's hut): 35 items a day, shared out by sliders among berries, herbs, mushrooms, plant fiber, stone, copper ore and tin ore.
    - Woodcutter's Hut: 12 wood and 8 sticks.
    - A hungry worker makes half.
    - **Stone and ores:** the separate Stone Gatherer's Hut is gone; stone, copper and tin come from the Forager's Hut. A save's old Stone Gatherer's Hut loads as a Forager's Hut if it meets one's requirements (renamed if it still had the old name), otherwise the plot is dropped and its pieces stay standing, owned by no plot; either way a message says which (`PlotRegistry._settle_old_plots`). The Stone Sorting Table stays as furniture, decoration only now.
    - **Later:** iron and coal will come from a mine that villagers work at.
    - What won't fit in either (no free slot for a new kind of item; slots hold any amount) is lost, with a message: "Forager's Hut: Town storage full (3 herbs lost)" ("chest full" with no working Storehouse).
    - All three workplaces require a storage chest of their own, so a working one always has somewhere for its output. Should one somehow have none, the day's output is lost, with a message.
    - The amounts are data on each plot type (`daily_output`, `split_output_total`, `split_output`).
  - **Forager sliders:** a Production section in the Forager's Hut plot panel has sliders for berries, herbs, mushrooms, plant fiber, stone, copper ore and tin ore, always adding up to 100%. Moving one shares the rest among the others in their current proportions. Each shows the resulting amount per day. The default is 30/12/12/6/24/8/8 (11 berries, 4 herbs, 4 mushrooms, 2 plant fiber, 8 stone, 3 copper ore, 3 tin ore), saved with the plot; a save's older four-item split starts again from that default. The other workplaces' Production section lists their fixed output.
  - **Food (at dusk, 0.74):** each settler eats 2 food, berries first, then mushrooms.
    - It comes from town storage first, then the nearest storage chest of a working plot that has any, then any other storage chest inside a plot (working or not). Chests outside every plot are the player's personal chests: settlers never eat from them.
    - A settler who can't eat their fill is Hungry, shown as "Hungry (n days)" (or "Fed") in their panel and "(hungry)" in the plot tracker and plot panel.
    - Hungry settlers produce half. After 3 hungry days in a row they leave the settlement, walking off toward the valley's edge, with a message. Quest villagers never leave: hungry, they stop working altogether until they're fed.
    - A House's tracker shows "Food stored: n days": the food in town storage and every storage chest inside a plot, for everyone in the settlement, since they all eat from the same stores.
  - **Debug:** F10 (debug builds only; the developer console's `day`) skips to the next dawn: tonight's meal first if it hasn't happened yet, then the day's output and any arrival. `skip_days <n>` skips several.
  - **Saved:** name, look, home, job, position, hungry days and the quest flag; lingering visitors (with their background and when they leave), when the next visitor comes and how many quest villagers have come; the slider percentages and housing setting with each plot; which day's dawn and dusk have been handled.
- **Free build (plot):** a plain plot for the player's own building, first in the build menu's Buildings tab, unlocked with the hammer like the House. No requirements, no workers, no beds for villagers (it isn't housing), never "needing attention" in the Village screen (its status is a neutral "Free build", in its panel, the plot tracker and the Village screen), and it isn't announced as working. It moves like any plot, with its own pieces, so a big build of the player's own can be moved. (There was never a free-build or personal plot type before this; players used a House plot to move their own builds.)
- **Change type** (the plot panel's More menu): a plot becomes another unlocked type, keeping all its pieces (a House plot holding the player's own build becomes Free build). Types it's too small for, or a second Village Center, are listed but disabled, saying why. Its requirements are checked again for the new type; its name follows if it still had its old type's name ("House 2" becomes "Free build"); residents move out if it's no longer a house, workers past the new type's places lose the job; a Storehouse's share of town storage moves to the rest.
- **Village Center (plot):** one per village (a second can't be marked out, by hand or from a template). It needs the Bell Mast and no roof, and unlocks once the village has a working Storehouse (a new unlock kind, "Plot": a plot type working in the village). Its thumbnail in the build menu and plot panel is the concept painting (`assets/village_center/village_center_concept.jpg`). Its plot panel lists the current visitors.
  - **Bell Mast:** a Meshy model (`assets/village_center/bell_mast/`), 7 m tall with its yardarms 2.53 m across, placed like furniture (on floors and the ground, free 22.5° turns, on the floor's grid). Solid as its plinth (1.83 × 0.26 × 1.38 m) and its post (0.3 m square, up to 7 m); the yardarms and bell overhead aren't solid. Unlocked with the Village Center. Three distance levels: the full model (15,474 triangles) to 25 m, 3,842 triangles to 70 m, 968 beyond.
  - **Day counter fix:** sleeping from before midnight now counts as the next day.
- **Defense:** walls, watchtowers, and steam-powered defenses against Hollow raids.
- **Clockwork guardians:** late game, reprogrammed construct cores become defenders, turning the Empire's weapons against its ruins.
- **Terraforming:** a craftable, coal-fuelled steam shovel raises, lowers, and flattens ground for foundations, ponds, and channels from the river. Height-based only, so no caves or tunnels. It needs a save system so changes persist.

## Building system

The player builds freely with Valheim-style snap pieces, and plots turn those structures into working buildings. Free building stays closer to RuneScape: Dragonwilds than to Medieval Dynasty's set build stages.

**Pieces**

- **Building visuals:** pieces are rustic geometry built in code (planks, shakes, logs, stacked stone, rope) in weathered grey-brown, matching the cabin reference image. Furniture uses Meshy models fitted to exact boxes.
- **Snap pieces:** foundations, walls, floors, roof sections, stairs and doors that snap to each other corner to corner, as in Valheim (see Placing pieces, below).
- **Round builds:** curved wall arcs (22.5° and 45°) that close into full circles, round tower kits (circular floors, cone roofs, spiral stairs), and 22.5° rotation steps (1° steps with snapping turned off, Z).
- **Not voxels:** Enshrouded-style smooth voxel building was ruled out as too big for a first project, and it would not fit the existing terrain.
- **Built so far (first version):** wooden pieces in the hammer's build menu, under tabs (Foundations, Floors, Walls, Roofs, Beams & Pillars, Stairs & Doors, Round, Furniture, Paths, Other, Buildings; Q and R change tab, E closes it). Square pieces (2 m grid, 1 m half step): wood and stone foundations (solid blocks 3 m tall, their top 1 m above the ground and 2 m into it, stacking and snapping side by side; floors, walls and stairs snap onto their tops like onto floors), floor, Wall 2 m (2 m wide and 2 m high, the Standard family's wall) and Tall Wall 2.5 m (the Tall family's), doorway walls (Tall: opening 1.2 m wide and 2.2 m tall, so you walk through with the door open; Standard: 1.2 × 1.8 m) and their doors (E opens and closes them), window walls (Tall and Standard), 26° roof (rises 1 m over 2 m), stairs, and wood and stone pillars (on floor and foundation corners and edge middles, stacking; floors, walls and roofs sit on their tops, for platforms and porches). Triangles combine with the squares: a right triangle (half a square, long side 2.83 m) and a 45° triangle (two 2 m sides at 45°, short side 1.53 m, filling the gap between squares turned 45°, so squares and triangles make an octagon), each as a floor and a wood and stone foundation, with long (2.83 m) and short (1.53 m) walls for their odd sides. *Round* (one ring, radius 2.61 m, so a 45° arc spans 2 m and eight close a circle 5.2 m across): wood and stone round foundation wedges, curved walls (45° and 22.5°), round floor wedges and cone roof wedges (eighths), and 22.5° round floor and foundation wedges (sixteenths) to match the 22.5° walls. A round wedge's curved edge lies exactly under the curved walls' middle line. Every piece unlocks with the first planks. Pieces are placed and snapped as described under Placing pieces, below; free, they go wherever the crosshair is, sitting on the ground or on other pieces, and may float. Tab while placing opens the build menu: picking something switches to it (at the same turn), and closing it goes back to what you were placing. Pieces may overlap anything (see Overlapping, below). Demolishing is direct: with the hammer out, placing or not, pressing the middle mouse button on a piece makes it glow red, and letting go removes it for a full refund with a short message ("Demolished Wall (+4 Planks)"); looking off it before letting go cancels, and placing carries on afterwards with the same piece and turn. Holding X on a piece does the same for mice without a middle button (it glows red with a percentage while held; letting go early cancels). Nothing glows or gets removed otherwise. What won't fit in a full backpack is left in a bundle on the ground, picked up with E. Under a roof counts as shelter; a closed room (floor, walls, roof, door shut) stops the cold entirely, round rooms included. Floors and foundations keep the grass out. No structural support yet. Shapes: `building/pieces/piece_shapes.gd`; each piece is a `PieceBuildable` in `buildables/structure/` or `buildables/round/`, and can show a real model instead of the plain shape (`model_scene`). Wood pieces are textured with Poly Haven's Wood Planks Dirt and stone pieces with Rock Wall 13 (2K copies; 4K originals in `assets/textures/hd/`, outside git), mapped at real-world scale (one copy covers 2 m) in a frame that follows the building, so planks run on across neighbouring pieces without seams: level on walls, along floors, down the slope on roofs and overhangs, round the curve on curved walls and round floors, along beams and up posts (`building/pieces/piece_surface.gdshader`). Each piece gets a slight colour and brightness of its own, and darker grime toward its edges and the foot of upright faces. That plain textured box is now only the distance version (beyond 60 m). Close up, every piece is built in code from real parts in warm weathered grey-brown timber (around #7d705f; the wood texture's light blotches flattened, its dark grain kept, each plank's narrow sides and bevelled edges darkened), tan-grey stone (around #8a8272, dirtier lower down) and thick brown hemp rope (around #8b6f47) (`building/pieces/piece_detail.gd`, `piece_detail.gdshader`): walls of separate bevelled horizontal planks (random thickness, height, tilt, ends and gaps) over a dark core, chunky plank frames round doorways and windows, a door of vertical planks with iron strap hinges and a rope pull, gables of planks with a king post and brace, floors of rough planks with gaps, wood foundations of stacked logs, stone foundations and pillars of irregular bevelled blocks with dark mortar, stairs of plank treads on log stringers, and roofs, corners, eaves and overhangs of decking under overlapping rows of split shakes (a few out of place), with rafter tails under the eaves and fascia and barge boards round overhangs. Each shape has 8 versions, picked by the piece's id, so a piece looks the same after loading and neighbours differ. Log corner posts with rope lashings stand wherever walls meet at an angle, a log with rope ties runs along every ridge where two roofs meet (unless a ridge cap is there), and every foundation edge not shared with another foundation gets a lip of stone (or logs) reaching 12 cm past it, so walls sit on top with stone showing; all three are worked out from the pieces once they've stopped changing for 0.2 s (one rebuild after a template's ghosts all go in, not one a frame) and aren't saved (`building/pieces/joint_details.gd`). Pieces keep their exact size, corners and collision. Furniture keeps its own look. Every piece has an id and an owner plot, listed in the `PieceRegistry`, which also saves them.
- **The small kit (Valheim-style):** a 1×1 m floor; Tall Wall 1 m (1 m wide, 2.5 m high) and half walls (2 m and 1 m wide; Standard 1 m high, Tall 1.25 m); gables (wall triangles) for the 26° and 45° roofs, standing on the wall under a roof end; a 45° roof, and for each pitch an inner corner (valley), outer corner (hip) and ridge cap; straight beams (2 m, 4 m) and diagonal beams (26° and 45°, each as long as its triangle's sloped edge, and Long Diagonal Beams twice that for 4 m spans; all with their top on the line between their ends) and poles (1 m, 2 m, 4 m), which snap by their ends; pillars and half pillars (wood and stone; Standard 2 m and 1 m, Tall 2.5 m and 1.25 m); ladders (Standard 2 m, Tall 2.5 m; hold W while facing one to climb); railings (2 m, 1 m; 1 m tall) and a 2 m fence (1 m tall, centred on its line). (The quarter wall and the 1 m beam are gone; see the grid standard.) All snap by their corners (see Placing pieces). Roof panels have both ends cut plumb, so two roofs back to back meet in a sharp, closed peak (the ridge cap still sits over it) and the eave end reads as a cut rafter end; existing roofs pick this up when loaded. Overhangs, for each pitch (Roofs tab): an eave (2 m wide, 0.5 m out, carrying the roof's slope on outward and down; 1 plank, 1 plant fiber) for a roof's or hip corner's low edge, a gable overhang (0.5 m wide, 2 m along the slope; 1 plank, 1 plant fiber) for a roof's side edge, past the gable wall, and an eave corner (0.5 × 0.5 m; 1 plank) under a gable overhang's low end where it meets the eaves. They match the roof's thickness and plumb cuts, have a fascia board along their outer edges, tuck over wall tops and gables, and don't count as roof for shelter.
- **Heights that line up:** each family lines up within itself (see the grid standard, below). Standard: a wall 2 m, half walls 1 m; the 26° gable rises 1 m and the 45° gable (the 45° triangle wall) 2 m above what it stands on, so the 45° triangle stands beside a 2 m wall flush, its peak level with the wall's top, and a 26° gable rises one half wall. - **Overlapping (Valheim-style freedom):**
  - **The player doesn't fight the building system.** Placing pieces, furniture and the workbench, chest and campfire, laying out templates, building ghosts and moving plots all work the same way:
    - **Overlap anything:** they may overlap or clip into other buildings' pieces, furniture, workbenches, chests and campfires, plots, trees and rocks, snapped or free, without turning red.
    - **Any depth:** pieces sink into the ground as far as you like, with no note.
    - **People glide aside:** you or a settler standing where something lands don't stop it: it goes down, and whoever is inside it glides (a quarter of a second) to the nearest open spot beside it: the nearest place out to 8 m where a person fits standing on the ground or a floor, no more than 2 m up or down, never on top of what was just put down (`building/people_mover.gd`).   - **Templates:** pieces that would exactly repeat ones already there (a wall shared with the building beside it) are left out when the template is placed.
  - **Warnings (yellow, still placeable):** steep ground under a template's foundations or under a moved plot, and steep ground or water under a workbench, chest or campfire.
  - **Physics:** placed pieces are still solid to walk into, and settlers still path round them.
  - **No flicker:** each piece's look sits up to 2.5 mm off its true place, in a level direction of its own (from its id; none for floors, foundations, flights and roofs), so faces of overlapping pieces that lie in the same plane don't flicker.

**Plots**

- **Chosen from a list** of available building types (house, forge, storehouse). Each type has requirements, such as workplace items or housing furniture, shown as a checklist. The building works only when they are met; extra decoration is up to the player.
- **Adjustable at any time:** plots can be resized and moved whenever the player likes. The building re-checks its requirements after every change and stops working (NPCs included) if something required ends up outside.
- **Overlap allowed:** plots can overlap so buildings can connect. Pieces may overlap too (see Overlapping).
- **Ownership:** every piece belongs to the plot it was placed in, even inside an overlap, and only its own pieces count toward its requirements.
- **Moving a building** takes only its own pieces. A gap left in a connected building is rebuilt by hand.
  - **Starting it:** with the hammer out, aim at a plot (its ground or one of its pieces; the controls row shows "Plot: move, resize… [E]"), press E for its panel, then Move plot (or M). In plot view E always opens the plot; outside it, a door, chest or the Storehouse's storage under the crosshair does its own thing first.
  - **Moving:** the plot and all of its own pieces (ghosts too) lift into a see-through preview that follows the crosshair, turning about its middle: the wheel 5° a notch, Alt + wheel 1°, R back to how it stood (and Page Up / Page Down raise and lower it: see below). Left-click puts it down; right-click or Esc cancels and puts it back. Pieces of other plots stay where they are, even joined to it.
  - **Raising and lowering:** Page Up / Page Down raise and lower the whole building (plot, pieces and furniture together): 0.25 m a press, 0.05 m with Alt, no limit either way; Backspace puts it back where it would sit (its middle on the ground). The panel shows "Turn 30° · Height +0.75 m". The height carries into where it's put down; settlers keep their jobs and beds, which move with it. Measured through the real plot placer: the Basic House (109 things) moved onto 33° ground and raised 0.75 m came down with its middle exactly 0.750 m above the ground (Backspace showed +0.00 m on the way), every piece, bed included, moved together to within 0.1 mm; the Forager's Hut (155 things) lowered 0.5 m came down exactly 0.500 m into the ground, every piece, basket and chest together to within 0.1 mm. Both kept their id and kept working.
  - **Nothing blocks it:** its pieces may overlap other buildings (pieces, furniture, workbenches, chests, campfires) and plot outlines, and sink into the ground as far as you like; anyone standing where it lands glides aside (see Overlapping). Steep ground is a yellow warning.
  - **After:** its id stays, so settlers keep their jobs and beds; corner posts, ridge logs and lips are worked out again, and settlers' paths follow.
  - **Measured** through the real plot placer, on the day-4 save: the Forager's Hut (155 things of its own) moved 10 m and turned 30° (6 wheel notches): every piece ended up offset and turned together (0.0 mm, 0.04° from exact), none of the 271 other plots' pieces moved, and it was still working afterwards. (Since then nothing refuses a move: the Basic House moved onto the Storehouse and 8° ground placed.)
- **Resizing:** in plot view (P), aim at a plot's edge or corner (it lights up; the controls row shows "Resize plot [Drag LMB]") and drag. The plot panel's hint says so.
- **Shrinking a plot:** pieces that would fall outside glow red. Confirming shows a warning, e.g. "6 pieces will be removed and refunded to your storage. Proceed?" (Proposed destination: warehouse, else nearest chest with room, else a crate dropped at the plot.)

**Templates**

- **Build it yourself or use a template.** A placed template becomes ordinary pieces the player can change.
- **Placing:** the template shows fully built with real textures, plus a green outline glow (yellow for steep ground; nothing makes it red).
- **Confirming:** it turns into blue ghost outlines that show each piece's cost, plus overall progress (e.g. "Forge: 14/32 pieces").
- **Filling:** the player hits a ghost piece with the hammer while carrying its materials. Building uses only what is in the player's inventory, not storage. Ghost pieces can be removed or swapped for free before they are built.
- **Player templates:** players save their own builds as templates.

    - The player's own templates can always be changed. Built-in ones only in debug builds, where it edits the files in `buildables/templates/`; never in a release build, where the ✕ doesn't show and right-clicking says they can't be changed.
    - Plots and buildings already placed from a template don't depend on its file, so deleting or renaming it leaves them as they are.
  - **Placing:** the whole building follows the crosshair, fully built with its real look and a soft outline glow, with its plot's outline on the ground.
    - It glows green; never red (it's laid out as ghosts, which nobody can be in the way of, and it may overlap anything); yellow (a warning, still placeable) where the ground under its foundations is steeper than 30° or it would stand in water. Pieces that exactly repeat ones already there are left out.
    - R turns it 90° and Alt + wheel 15°; Page Up / Page Down raise and lower it (Alt: finely); Backspace resets the turn and height.
    - Near another building it snaps where one of its outer snap points meets one of theirs, by the usual snap rules at its current turn.
    - Left-click creates the plot (the template's type and size, adjustable afterwards in plot view) with everything in it as a ghost. Placing costs nothing.
  - **Ghosts:** each ghost shows its simplified look (the ghost under the crosshair its full detailed look: every plank, shake and rafter; furniture and the workbench, chest and campfire their models) in its real material, faded and desaturated toward a pale cool grey, solid and casting no shadow, so it stays clearly apart from built pieces. The ghost under the crosshair (the one you'd fill) fades only half as much and gets a thin light outline. Code: the pieces' shaders' `ghost_fade`, `BuildPiece.set_ghost` and `building/preview_look.gd` (ghost buildings' models, which can't fade themselves, get a faded overlay instead).
    - **Rules:** ghosts aren't solid (the player, settlers and every physics check pass through them), and don't count as built for requirements, shelter, settlers, joint details or fires.
    - **Aiming:** aim at a ghost with the hammer and it brightens, and the placement panel shows its name and cost (red if you're short).
    - **Building:** left-click builds it from your inventory only, turning it into the real piece, workbench, chest or campfire (anyone standing in it glides aside).
    - **Holding:** holding left-click keeps going, about 4 a second, with the ghost under the crosshair and then the nearest ghosts of the same plot joined to what's been built this hold, until materials run out.
    - **Removing:** middle-click removes a ghost for free.
    - **Alongside:** normal pieces can still be placed in the plot alongside ghosts. Removing a plot removes its ghosts; moving or resizing it takes them along.
    - **Tracker:** while a plot has ghosts, its tracker says "Under construction" and shows "Building: 14/28 pieces", then each material needed, carried and missing.
    - **Finishing:** when the last ghost is built: "House is finished!", and the usual requirement checks take over.
  - **Saved:** ghosts save and load with their plot (ghost pieces as a flag on the piece, ghost buildings with the buildings).

**Storage and builders**

- **Chests:** the player's personal storage. NPCs never touch the player’s personal chests (those outside any plot); chests inside plots are settlement storage.
- **Warehouse:** town storage the player can open. NPCs drop their daily output here.
- **Built so far (town storage, first version):** the Storehouse is the warehouse. Code: `building/plots/town_storage.gd` (data: `PlotType.town_storage_item` and `town_storage_slots`).
  - **Size:** a working Storehouse (one with its Storehouse Storage) gives 60 slots (a slot holds any amount of one item, as in a chest), and each extra Storehouse adds 60 to the shared pool, the town storage. Storage shelves are decoration only.
  - **Tracker:** a Storehouse's tracker shows "Town storage: 14/24 slots" for the whole pool, or "closed until it's working".
  - **Opening it:** E on the Storehouse Storage in a working Storehouse ("Open town storage") opens the Town Storage screen beside your inventory, in the chest screen's style:
    - the whole pool, sorted by category (food, materials, tools, other), with the slots in use in the title and each category's total;
    - drag or shift-click to move stacks across;
    - Deposit resources moves every material and food item from your inventory into it, leaving tools and the action bar alone.
    - It scrolls past three rows. A shelf in a Storehouse that isn't working says so.
  - **Settlers:** job output at dawn goes into town storage while it has room, otherwise into the hut's own chest. What fits in neither is lost: "Forager's Hut: Town storage full (3 herbs lost)". Settlers eat from town storage first, then from plot chests as before, and a House's "Food stored" counts it.
  - **Refunds:** shrinking a plot refunds its removed pieces into town storage first, then your inventory, then a bundle at your feet. Demolishing by hand still refunds to your inventory. Building (placing pieces, filling ghosts) still takes only what you carry.
  - **Saved:** with the Storehouse plots, each holding a share of the pool.
  - **Shrinking, so nothing is lost:**
    - A Storehouse that stops working (missing requirements) keeps its share. This includes older Storehouses built before Storehouse Storage existed, which show "Missing requirements" until it's built. The pool then holds more than its room and takes no new kinds of item (and no new stock at all while no Storehouse works) until there's room again. Settlers can still eat the food in it.
    - Only removing a Storehouse pushes stock out: whatever the remaining Storehouses couldn't hold drops in a bundle where it stood, with a message.
- **Granary (later):** a separate food store with spoilage.
- **Builder NPCs** fill ghost pieces from warehouse stock. First version: materials come straight from the warehouse. Later, once NPCs can walk around the town, builders carry loads from the warehouse to the site.

## Interface style

Every panel follows one style guide, kept in code so it can't drift: the palette, sizes and widgets in `ui/brass_style.gd` (BrassStyle), and the standard panel in `ui/brass_panel.gd` (BrassPanel). The brass plaque frames stay.

- **Panel layout** (BrassPanel):
  - **Header:** the subject's name (an editable name where it can be renamed, like a plot's), a status pill on the right, a small subtitle under it (size; "Leaves in about a day"), and badges if a panel ever needs them.
  - A thin brass divider.
  - **Body:** sections, each with a small uppercase brass heading (WORKER, PRODUCTION · EACH DAWN, REQUIREMENTS 2/3); two-column key/value facts (Home, Job, Food, Doing) instead of "Home: …" sentences; two-column checklists.
  - A divider.
  - **Action row:** the primary action far right, other actions beside it, a danger action far left on its own.

    - **Used per day:** tomorrow's planned use by the villagers (the food dusk's meals will eat, berries first). The player's own takings never count; they just lower what's in storage. (Nothing else uses materials yet; workstation and villager building use will add here.)

Town storage's room is counted in slots of any amount, not per item, so it shows once above the groups ("Town storage 12/60 slots") rather than as amount / capacity on each row.

    - **Detail panel:** clicking a row opens it at the screen's right edge, and the ledger moves to the left edge so the row stays in view (click another row to switch; E or Esc goes back to the ledger; V closes the screen). ## World and look

The valley is built from real-world elevation data and is heading toward a realistic natural look, with steampunk ruins layered on top.

- **No orange for highlights, indicators, warnings or status colours** (previews, outlines, glows, selection, alerts). Exceptions: brass and gold interface materials, fire and light, sunlight, and object icons and markers. Warnings are a clear yellow, neutral highlights white, errors red (placing: a soft white glow where a piece can go, yellow for a warning, red where it can't; a plot's missing materials, the plot tracker's and the plot outline's, yellow; the action-slot glow white).

- **Terrain:** a heightmap of rolling hills in the English Midlands (36 to 226 m real elevation), built in chunks. Currently 1500 m across with the hills exaggerated. The rest of the heightmap is room to expand, and airship travel can open new regions later.
- **Water:** a single water level fills the low ground, forming the river and lake.
- **Paths:** the editor's Terrain Paths and the dirt paths players paint look the same. They show plain worn earth (ambientCG Ground109, the light dry soil), with darker damp patches (Ground048) and a few loose stones in small clusters down the middle (Ground108). In woods the edges turn to forest floor. The earth gives way to the grass over a wide, ragged margin, height blended so tufts break into it, so there's no hard line. The old sandy pebble surface (Ground049C) stood out too much; its files are kept.
- **Path tiles (the player's paths):** the build menu's Paths tab has Dirt, Gravel, Stone and Remove. Paths are ground paint, not objects: no collision, nothing blocks them, and buildings, plots and furniture go over them freely.
  - **Placing (drag):** paths are the one piece sized by dragging (CLAUDE.md). Press left click where the strip starts, drag, let go where it ends (within 16 m of the camera). A strip is always 2 m wide, at any angle, its length in 0.5 m steps (0.5 m at least). Its outline shows on the ground while dragging (before pressing, a circle where it would start): white, red for Remove or when you can't pay. Right click or Esc cancels the drag, then the tool. With snapping on (Z), either end snaps to a spot within 0.8 m of the crosshair on any path type: a strip's end (so a winding path is a chain of straight strips), or beside an end on the 1 m step out to 4 m (side by side for a wider road, or half over for an offset layered border). Overlapping is always allowed. Where a strip starts or ends on the end of a strip of its own type, both ends there are made round, so bends have no gap or notch. Remove works the same way: drag over a strip (its ends snap to the strip's) to clear every type under it back to grass. No collision, nothing blocks building.
  - **Layer order:** each type has a rank (Dirt 1, Gravel 2, Stone 3, room for a fourth). A higher rank always draws on top, whatever order they were laid in.
  - **Edges:** every edge stays on its strip with a narrow ragged worn fray: dirt reaches about 5 cm past the strip (10 to 20 cm at most with its fray), gravel stops about 3 cm short, laid stone 10 cm short with a firmer edge. So dirt, gravel and stone side by side or offset still leave a worn layered border. Each strip's edge also wanders up to 4 cm in a pattern laid in the world (0.6 m across), so neighbours wander together and meet without a seam; the shader adds a fine fray (3 to 5 cm, in patterns 0.25 to 0.33 m across).
    - Why paths used to spread into blotches (fixed): not the map's resolution. The old edges reached far past the tile: 0.3 m spread, ±0.22 m baked wander, a 0.25 m soft edge and up to ±0.55 m of shader noise in lobes about 5 m across, so a 2 m dirt tile showed as a 3 to 4 m splotch; the grass cleared about 0.6 m past it too. The map (0.25 m a pixel, 8-bit distance over 2 m: 8 mm steps) draws a straight edge exactly, so no finer map is needed. (The Texture Brush paint map is 1 m a pixel; paths don't use it.)
  - **Grass and ferns** are cleared off paths and grow back where they're removed.
  - **Unlocks and costs** (per 4 m of strip; a strip pays its share, each item rounded up, at least 1):
    - Dirt: from the start, free.
    - Gravel: unlocks when you craft a pickaxe; 2 Stone.
    - Stone paving: unlocks with a working Storehouse, for now (later: the Forge era); 4 Stone.
    - Remove: free, no refund.
  - **How it's kept:** the strips as laid, in order, each as six 32-bit numbers (rank, its two ends' x and z, and which ends are round), with Remove strips among them (save version 2). The ground is worked out from that list the same way every time, so a save loads exactly (checked byte for byte). Laying the same strip twice (either way round) changes nothing, and a Remove laid exactly over a strip takes that strip out of the list (keeping the Remove only if other strips reach under it), so the list stays about as long as the paths on the ground. Older saves' 2 × 4 m tiles (version 1: rank, x, z, turn) load as the same strips, square at both ends (their edges now draw tighter, as intended).
  - **Drawn:** a map like the painted paths' (a pixel per 0.25 m, 32 m tiles, only where there's path), with a channel per rank holding how far inside or outside that type's path each spot is, so strips merge seamlessly and a Remove cuts its shape out of all of them. The ground shader turns each channel into its type's edge and draws them in rank order after the Texture Brush paint. The textures (`assets/textures/paths/dirt`, `gravel`, `stone`: ambientCG Ground103, Ground110, Bricks089) are three of the ground's texture sets: 13 of the 16 slots are now used. They have no Displacement maps, so their height comes from their AO map.
  - `tests/path_shots.tscn` lays a winding dirt path (three strips, two bends) and a road three strips wide (dirt edges, gravel inside, stone down the middle), measures the dirt's reach past its strip, checks Remove, save/load and old saves, measures the save size, and pictures them into `docs/reference/ours/paths/`.
- **Painted dirt paths (retired):** the free-hand Dirt Path tool is no longer in the build menu (`retired` on its PathTool); what it painted in older saves still shows, saves and keeps the grass off.
  - Hold left click and drag to paint a soft-edged strip onto the ground under the crosshair (within 14 m). It starts 1.5 m wide; the wheel changes the width from 1 to 3 m in 0.25 m steps, and a ring on the ground shows the brush. Middle click or holding X erases; right click or Esc puts the tool away.
  - Controls row: Paint [LMB] · Width [Wheel] · Erase [MMB] · Cancel [RMB].
  - It never changes the ground's height. Grass and ferns go where it's painted and don't regrow there; erased path grows its grass back.
  - Paths are kept in a fine map (a pixel per 0.25 m) in 32 m tiles, only where something's painted. The ground shader reads the tiles from a texture array, and painting uploads only the tiles that changed, so dragging causes no lag spikes. They're saved with the game, compressed (a 10 m path is about half a kilobyte).
  - Later: villagers may prefer walking on paths.
  - `tests/path_regression.tscn` checks the tool end to end: painting 10 m through grass, erasing half, saving and loading, the controls row, and frame times while dragging (and now that it's no longer in the menu).
- **Visual target:** a realistic forest-and-stream look like the reference screenshot (rocky slopes, ferns, deer). Lighting, fog, shaders, and procedural grass come first; real art assets (Poly Haven, ambientCG, asset packs) replace placeholders in a later beauty pass.
- **Places to discover:** the ruined airship dock, an abandoned workshop, and rusting machinery in the hills.
- **Campsites:** abandoned refugee camps spawn at random around the map, with new ones appearing over time in unexplored areas.
  - They hold schematic fragments, loot and journal pages, and later dangers (wolves, then Hollows).
  - **Role still to decide.** (Settlers now come from the castle quest and from visitors to the Village Center, not from campsites.)
- **Map shape and border:** the playable area doesn't need to be square. Natural borders such as steep hills, cliffs, a gorge, or fog mark the edge, with an invisible wall behind them as a backstop. Beyond the border, low-detail terrain with no foliage or collision keeps the world looking continuous.
- **World map:** an overhead render of the terrain, styled like an old hand-drawn chart, with markers for the player and discovered vents (proposed).

Each piece's own slight colour comes from the shader. Corner posts, ridge logs and foundation lips share a few versions per size the same way.
  - **Three looks by distance:** the full rustic look within 15 m; a simplified version (the same planks, logs and stones with plain edges, each row of shakes as one strip, fewer-sided logs, no rope) out to 40 m; the plain textured box beyond. Each fades into the next over 2 m (Godot visibility ranges).
  - **Hybrid wood walls** (`PieceRegistry.is_hybrid`): the wood wall family, Standard and Tall, works differently. That covers walls, half walls, strips, doorways, windows, gables and triangle walls.
    - Looks: modelled planks within 15 m; beyond that, a slab (the piece's plain box) wearing textures baked from its own modelled planks. There's no middle look.
    - The switch: instant at 15 m, with no fade, no dithering and no margin. Both looks share one box, so exactly one shows at any distance. 15 m was measured against 8 and 12 m (`tests/look_pass.gd`): the switch shows least there, and the planks just inside it shimmer only a little more than the slab.
    - The bake (`building/pieces/wall_baker.gd`): each shape's 4 plank variants are drawn straight on, unlit, by an orthographic camera (`wall_bake.gdshader`): once for colour, once for the surface (the normal, with the close-up normal map, plus how deep each face sits, which darkens the sky light in the gaps). They're drawn at twice the size and shrunk to 256 pixels a metre (512 across a 2 m wall), mipmapped and compressed (DXT1). The slab (`wall_far.gdshader`) lays them on by the piece's own X and Y, front and back, so every board, joint, stagger, shade and frame lines up across the switch.
    - When it bakes: every shape is prepared while the world opens, under the "Getting the valley ready…" cover (`ShaderWarmup`), so nothing bakes or loads mid-game. Shapes are read from `user://wall_bakes/` when cached. Otherwise they're baked, and the cover counts down the shapes left while the player is held still. A change to the look code (the files in `WallBaker.SOURCES`) clears the cache, and everything is baked again. All 128 bakes (32 shapes × 4 variants) take about 10 s the first time and about 29 MB of video memory. Until its bake is ready, a wall shows its close-up planks at every distance, and switches over when the bake is in. Every piece registry makes its baker when it's created, so registries outside the world (the icon updater's) work too. They don't bake until they're in the tree, and headless runs never bake. `tests/startup_test.gd` opens the real title screen and loads a save, and fails on any error at start-up.
    - **Later:** pre-bake the wall and roof bake cache when the game is exported, and ship it with the game, so players never see the first-launch bake (about 37 s on a cold start now).
    - Shadows: one rule for every piece with a far look (hybrid walls and roofs, `BuildPiece.far_shadows`). Its shadow comes from its far mesh at every distance, through a shadow-only copy, and none of its visible looks casts one, so shadows don't change at the switch. The copies share one material that casts from back faces only. A far mesh is a closed solid, so its surface facing the light lies behind the close-up planks or shakes, and those can't shadow themselves. Roofs' fascia, barge boards and rafters cast as part of the far mesh. The ridge logs along roof joins cast their own, as before.
  - **Hybrid roofs** (`WallBaker.ROOFS`, both pitches): roof panels, eaves, gable overhangs, inner and outer corners, hip ends, toppers and topper overhangs. Left out:
    - eave corners (half a metre square);
    - valley and outer eave corners (they have no close-up look);
    - ridge caps (only a log);
    - the cone roof (round family).
  - How hybrid roofs work:
    - Looks: modelled shakes within 25 m; beyond that, one far mesh per variant in the plain box's place. It's a thin slab over each shaked facet (top 0.135 m off the deck's underside, underside on it), with the roof's trim merged in as real geometry in plain colours: fascia and barge boards, rafters, hip and ridge logs. So the edges don't change at the switch, and a far roof is a single draw.
    - The bake: the close-up look is built in two layers (`PieceDetail.layer`): the deck and shakes, and the trim. Each layer is exactly its share of the whole look. Each shaked facet of the deck-and-shakes layer is baked straight on in its own plane, from above and from below, at 128 pixels a metre: 64 bakes (16 shapes × 4 variants), about 15 MB. The slab's UVs and tangents point straight into its tiles, so the baked normals act as a normal map, and the bake's depth darkens the sky light between shakes.
    - The switch: 25 m, measured against 12, 15 and 20 m from the ground (`tests/roof_pass.gd`). The difference falls steadily with distance, and the shakes never shimmer more than about 1.6× the far look. By eye the rows and staggers also line up when looking along the slope.
    - Like walls: baked with the walls' baker, cache and pre-bake under the loading cover. Close-up shakes show until the bake is in, and the shadow comes from the far mesh at every distance (the shadow rule above).
  - **Hybrid floors and foundations** (`WallBaker.FLOORS`, 16 shapes): floors (square, 1 m, both triangles, round wedges 45° and 22.5°) and wood and stone foundations (square, both triangles, both wedges).     - Looks: the modelled planks, flagstones, logs and stone courses within 15 m; beyond that, one far mesh per variant in the plain box's place, one draw. - Short freezes (what keeps the game from hitching):
  - **Grass and ferns:** each 32 m patch is worked out on a worker thread from a snapshot of what it needs (ground, clear zones, buildings), and finished patches are swapped in one per frame. A new floor or plot hides the plants under it straight away by flattening those instances in the stored patch, instead of regrowing the whole patch; removing one marks its patches to be regrown in the background.
  - **Saving:** the save's data is gathered on the main thread (a deep copy); turning it into text, writing the file, shrinking the picture and clearing away old autosaves happen on a worker thread. Manual saves wait for it to finish (the save list shows them straight after); quitting and the next save wait for any save still being written.
  - **Shader warm-up** (`building/pieces/shader_warmup.gd`): when a world opens, every building look (plain, close-up and simplified looks, their shadows, the distance fade, the template glow overlay, the faded and targeted ghosts, the placing preview's tints and outlines and the take-down glow) is drawn once for a few frames under a dark "Getting the valley ready…" cover, so the graphics card compiles those shaders then and not the first time you preview a template.
  - **Template preview:** its look is built about 100 nodes a frame and shown once it's all there ("Setting out the preview…" in the panel meanwhile; clicking before then finishes it at once). Its outer snap points are found with a 5 cm grid and kept per template. The snap search only looks at sockets the template wants, sorted into a 1 m grid; the physics check of where it stands runs every other frame (every frame or so while it's moving) once the look is complete. The shader warm-up also draws the preview's glow over fading looks, ghosts while fading, the plot outline and its floating label, and every workbench, chest and campfire templates hold.
  - **Laying out a template:** its ghosts go into the world 12 a frame; once they're all in, the plot takes in what stands inside it and its checklist is checked. Corner posts and ridge logs are rebuilt once, 0.2 s after the last piece goes in. Measured with the spike logger: no frame over 33 ms while laying one out (before: two of 34 and 35 ms, mostly those rebuilds).
  - **Respawns:** at most 6 things come back per check; when more are due (after a long sleep) the next check follows a tenth of a second later.
  - **Spike logger** (debug builds only, `dev/spike_log.gd`; Settings → Debug → "Log lag spikes", off by default): every frame over 33 ms prints a `[Spike]` line with the frame time, what the player was doing, the time spent in each timed system that frame (building, detail looks, saving, respawns, settlers and navigation, day and night, grass, plot checklists, menus), the nodes added and the shader pipelines compiled for the first time. Under it, the nodes added that frame and the one before (a node is first drawn, and its shaders compiled, in the render that falls in the next frame's time), by name and parent ("Detail (MeshInstance3D) ← Wall ×12"), and with shaders compiled, the kinds compiled (mesh, surface, canvas…) and the materials those nodes carry (Godot counts compiles but doesn't say which material each was for; "none" means something already there was drawn a new way). **T1 run-through checklist** (new-game audit, 12 Nov 2026: a fresh world walked from the crash site to the Village Center with shortcuts, `tests/new_game_audit.tscn`; no errors in the whole run)

What a new game is: the player stands 5 m from the wreck at 0.30 on day 0 with empty pockets and nothing in hand; the Ship's Log is 13 m away, 5 scrap by the wreck. Usable from the start: the campfire, the hand recipes (Hammer, Axe, Pickaxe, Knife) and, once there's a workbench, Planks, Rope, Torch and Spear. Stone (6 a pick-up), sticks (4) and plant fiber (6) lie within 10 m, berries 33 m, mushrooms 75 m, herbs 118 m, tin and copper ore 143 and 174 m; wood needs the axe (13 trees within 60 m, 3 wood each). A full day is 40 minutes (28 day, 12 night); a full belly lasts one full day (1.5× faster sprinting), a night's sleep costs 25, a berry gives 15; starving halves your speed and caps stamina at 50 but does no harm. Warmth is off.
